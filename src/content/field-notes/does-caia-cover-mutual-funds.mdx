## The 30-second answer

The CAIA Charter (the Chartered Alternative Investment Analyst designation, launched in 2002 by the CAIA Association) is, by its own definition, the credential for *alternatives*. Hedge funds, private equity, real assets, structured products, commodities, and now digital assets. Mutual funds are the textbook example of a *traditional* investment, which is exactly the bucket CAIA is trying to look beyond.

So if the question is "does CAIA teach me how to be a mutual fund analyst?" the answer is no. Go take the CFA for that.

But if the question is "do mutual funds appear in CAIA?" the answer is yes, and in three specific places:

1. **As the comparison set.** Every chapter on a hedge fund strategy is implicitly arguing "here's why this isn't just an expensive mutual fund."
2. **As a regulatory shell.** The Investment Company Act of 1940, the law that created the modern mutual fund, is also the law that governs "liquid alternatives," which CAIA covers explicitly.
3. **As a distribution channel.** A growing share of hedge fund-style strategies are now packaged inside 40 Act mutual funds and European UCITS funds so that retail investors can buy them. CAIA treats this as a core trend.

That third bucket is where most exam-relevant material lives. If you understand why a long/short equity manager would launch a mutual fund version of their hedge fund, and what they lose in the process, you've nailed the part of CAIA that touches mutual funds.

The rest of this guide walks through how we got here.

## Part 1: Where mutual funds came from

You can't understand why CAIA treats mutual funds as the "default" without understanding that the mutual fund itself was, a century ago, the radical alternative.

### March 21, 1924: Boston, State Street

The story starts with **Edward G. Leffler**, a Swedish-American from Wisconsin who sold aluminum pots and pans door-to-door after World War I before switching to selling securities. Leffler spent six years watching how ordinary people interacted with the closed-end investment trusts of the 1920s: vehicles that traded on exchanges, used leverage aggressively, and frequently traded at wide discounts or premiums to the value of what they actually held.

He thought there had to be a better version. His insight: what if investors could redeem their shares at any time, at the actual net asset value, and the fund could continuously issue new shares at that same NAV? No leverage. No discount-to-NAV games. Daily liquidity. Professional management. Affordable minimums.

He pitched the idea to several Boston brokerages and got turned down repeatedly. Then he walked into Learoyd, Foster & Co. on State Street. **Charles H. Learoyd** and **Hatherly Foster Jr.** said yes.

On March 21, 1924, the three of them launched **Massachusetts Investors Trust (MIT)**, the first open-end mutual fund in U.S. history. The minimum investment was $250 plus a 5% sales charge, which came to about $2.50 less than a new Ford Model T runabout. The fund still exists today, inside MFS Investment Management, under the ticker MITTX.

### The setup for 1940

By the end of 1929, there were 19 open-end mutual funds in the U.S. and roughly 700 closed-end funds. The 1929 crash wiped out most of the leveraged closed-end vehicles. The little open-end funds, with no leverage and daily redemptions, mostly survived.

That asymmetric survival rate is the reason the U.S. has the regulatory regime it has today. Congress passed the Securities Act of 1933, the Securities Exchange Act of 1934 (creating the SEC), and, most importantly for this discussion, the **Investment Company Act of 1940**, which set the rules for registered investment companies. That's the "40 Act" you'll see referenced constantly in CAIA materials.

The 40 Act is the rulebook for:

- Open-end mutual funds
- Closed-end funds
- Exchange-traded funds (ETFs)
- Unit Investment Trusts (UITs)

Anything you can buy through a normal brokerage account that pools money from retail investors is, almost certainly, a 40 Act fund.

### The Bogle revolution

Fast-forward to 1975. **John C. Bogle**, a Princeton graduate (his senior thesis was on mutual funds) who had been pushed out of Wellington Management after a bad merger, founded **The Vanguard Group** with an unusual structure: the fund company would be owned by the funds themselves, eliminating the conflict between fund shareholders and management company shareholders.

In 1976, Bogle launched the **First Index Investment Trust**, which would become Vanguard 500 Index Fund (VFINX), the first retail index mutual fund. It was mocked at the time as "Bogle's Folly." Today, index funds and ETFs hold the majority of U.S. equity fund assets.

A few other names from that era worth remembering, because they come up in passing in CAIA readings or in industry context you're expected to recognize:

<WrapTable>

| Person | Affiliation | What they're known for |
|---|---|---|
| Edward Leffler | Massachusetts Investors Trust | Inventing the open-end mutual fund (1924) |
| Paul Cabot | State Street Investment Corp. | Co-founded one of the next earliest U.S. open-end funds |
| Walter Morgan | Wellington Fund | First balanced (stock and bond) mutual fund (1928) |
| Jack Bogle | Vanguard | Founded Vanguard, popularized index investing |
| Peter Lynch | Fidelity Magellan | Star active manager, 29% annualized from 1977 to 1990 |
| Bill Miller | Legg Mason Value Trust | Beat the S&P 500 for 15 consecutive years (1991 to 2005) |

</WrapTable>

All of the above ran or invented mutual funds. None of them are at the center of the CAIA syllabus. That's the point.

## Part 2: Why CAIA exists in the first place

The Chartered Alternative Investment Analyst program was launched in 2002 by the CAIA Association, a joint effort of the Alternative Investment Management Association (AIMA) and the Center for International Securities and Derivatives Markets at UMass Amherst. It was a response to a specific institutional reality: by the late 1990s and early 2000s, pension funds, endowments, sovereign wealth funds, and family offices were allocating massive percentages of their portfolios to assets that the CFA curriculum barely touched.

The most famous example is the **Yale endowment under David Swensen**, who shifted Yale's portfolio dramatically toward private equity, hedge funds, and real assets starting in the 1980s, eventually reaching over 75% in alternatives. By the 2000s, every endowment CIO in America was trying to replicate "the Yale Model." But the analysts feeding those CIOs were trained on public market securities. There was a credential gap.

CAIA was designed to fill it. The Level I curriculum runs through:

1. Professional Standards and Ethics
2. Introduction to Alternative Investments
3. Real Assets
4. Private Securities (private equity, private credit, growth equity)
5. Hedge Funds
6. Digital Assets

Level II layers on portfolio construction, institutional asset ownership, due diligence, and rebalancing illiquid portfolios: the operational side.

Notice what's missing from both lists: a topic called "Mutual Funds."

That's not an oversight. It's the entire point of the credential.

## Part 3: Where mutual funds actually show up in CAIA

So if there's no chapter labeled "mutual funds," where are they?

### 3.1 As the implicit comparison

The CAIA Level I introductory readings define alternative investments largely by contrast to traditional ones. The taxonomy you'll see early in the curriculum splits the investable universe into something like:

<WrapTable>

| Traditional | Alternative |
|---|---|
| Public equities | Private equity |
| Investment-grade bonds | Private credit, distressed debt |
| Cash | Hedge funds, managed futures |
| Long-only mutual funds | Real assets, commodities, infrastructure |
| Index ETFs | Structured products, digital assets |

</WrapTable>

Every defining feature of alternatives (illiquidity premium, performance fees, lockups, accredited investor requirements, complex tax treatment) is defined by what it *isn't*: namely, a daily-liquidity, low-fee, retail-accessible mutual fund.

You can't fully understand why a hedge fund charges "2 and 20" without understanding why a Vanguard index fund charges 4 basis points. Mutual funds are the floor that alternatives have to justify themselves against.

### 3.2 As a regulatory wrapper: 40 Act funds and liquid alternatives

This is where mutual funds get real CAIA airtime.

A "liquid alternative," or "liquid alt," is an alternative investment strategy (long/short equity, managed futures, global macro, market neutral) delivered through a 40 Act mutual fund or ETF structure instead of a traditional hedge fund partnership. They're called 40 Act funds because they're registered under the Investment Company Act of 1940.

The growth here was fast, and then it wasn't. Launches of liquid alt funds **tripled between 2009 and 2013** in the aftermath of the financial crisis, when retail investors and their advisors wanted hedge-fund-style diversification but didn't want the gates, lockups, and accreditation requirements. U.S. liquid alternative assets went from about $124 billion in 2010 to about $310 billion in 2014. Then the boom stalled: 2015 brought almost no net new money, and dozens of funds closed.

What CAIA wants you to understand is the *structural cost* of putting a hedge fund strategy into a mutual fund wrapper. The 40 Act imposes three big constraints:

<WrapTable>

| Constraint | What the 40 Act requires | What the strategy loses |
|---|---|---|
| **Liquidity** | A 40 Act fund must keep at least 85% of its portfolio in liquid securities (sellable within 7 days) | No illiquidity premium. It can't hold the private credit, distressed debt, or thinly traded names that drive hedge fund alpha |
| **Shorting** | Strict limits on short exposure | Can't run a real long/short book the way an unconstrained hedge fund can |
| **Leverage** | Asset coverage requirements limit leverage to roughly 50% of net assets | Strategies like risk parity, fixed income arbitrage, or relative value lose a major lever |

</WrapTable>

The CAIA-relevant takeaway: liquid alts give up much of the alpha that hedge funds generate, in exchange for the daily liquidity, lower minimums, and 1099 tax reporting that mutual funds offer. Whether the trade is worth it depends on the strategy. Trend-following managed futures translate reasonably well into 40 Act form. Distressed credit and merger arbitrage suffer badly.

You should also know the European equivalent: **UCITS** funds (Undertakings for Collective Investment in Transferable Securities). UCITS is the EU's mutual fund regulatory regime, and "alternative UCITS" funds are the European liquid alts. The regulatory constraints are similar but not identical. UCITS allows somewhat more flexibility on derivatives, less on illiquid holdings.

### 3.3 As a distribution channel for alternative managers

The third place mutual funds show up in CAIA is in the conversation about how alternative asset managers grow. The big shift over the past 15 years is that traditional hedge funds and private equity firms have launched mutual fund and ETF products to reach retail wealth.

Examples worth knowing because they come up in industry context:

- **AQR Capital Management**, founded by Cliff Asness, runs both traditional hedge funds and a full lineup of liquid alt mutual funds (managed futures, style premia, market neutral).
- **Blackstone** launched **BREIT** (Blackstone Real Estate Income Trust), a non-traded REIT that brought private real estate to retail wealth channels. It is not technically a mutual fund, but it is a directly comparable retail-accessible alternative wrapper.
- **JPMorgan Asset Management** runs the Hedged Equity Fund (JHEQX), one of the largest liquid alt mutual funds at over $20 billion, which uses an options collar strategy on the S&P 500.
- **PIMCO**, originally a traditional fixed income manager, has run alternative mutual funds for over a decade.
- **BlackRock** runs both traditional iShares ETFs and a growing lineup of alternative ETFs and interval funds, often via its acquired GIP (Global Infrastructure Partners) franchise.

The pattern: every alternatives manager eventually has to decide whether to stay private-only or build out a retail-accessible product line in mutual fund and ETF form. That decision, and its consequences, is the kind of thing Level II asks about in portfolio construction and asset owner contexts.

### 3.4 Mutual fund structures inside institutional portfolios

One more place mutual funds show up: in the asset allocation discussions in Level II. Institutional investors don't only hold private partnerships. Endowments, pensions, and SWFs hold significant traditional equity and fixed income exposure, much of which is in commingled funds, mutual funds, or separately managed accounts. When CAIA discusses rebalancing illiquid portfolios, managing liquidity for capital calls, and constructing a complete institutional portfolio, the public-market sleeve, which is largely mutual-fund-like, is part of the picture.

## Part 4: A study framework

If you're prepping for CAIA and want to make sure you've got the mutual fund-related material covered, here's the mental model that works:

**Frame 1: Vehicles vs. strategies.**
A mutual fund is a *vehicle*. A hedge fund is *also* a vehicle (a private partnership). An ETF is a vehicle. A separately managed account is a vehicle. Long/short equity is a *strategy*. Managed futures is a strategy. Distressed credit is a strategy.

CAIA is primarily about strategies. But the same strategy can be delivered in different vehicles, and the vehicle determines the regulatory constraints, fees, liquidity profile, tax treatment, and investor access.

**Frame 2: The 40 Act is the constitution of retail investing.**
If something is sold to retail investors in the U.S., it almost certainly lives under the 40 Act. Know the three big constraints (liquidity, shorting, leverage) cold. They're the reason liquid alts can't fully replicate hedge fund performance.

**Frame 3: The institutional/retail divide is shrinking.**
The big trend CAIA wants you to see: alternative strategies that were once only available to institutions and ultra-high-net-worth investors are increasingly being packaged for retail through liquid alt mutual funds, interval funds, non-traded REITs, and now tokenized funds. Understand what's gained (access, liquidity) and lost (alpha, illiquidity premium) at each step down the access ladder.

## Part 5: Things to know cold for the exam

A quick rapid-fire of mutual fund-adjacent facts worth having ready for CAIA-style questions:

- The Investment Company Act of 1940 governs the registered fund types you will meet most often: open-end funds (mutual funds), closed-end funds, ETFs, and unit investment trusts.
- A 40 Act fund must maintain at least 85% of its portfolio in liquid securities.
- UCITS is the European mutual fund regulatory regime, governing roughly the EU equivalent of 40 Act funds.
- U.S. liquid alternative assets grew from about $124 billion in 2010 to about $310 billion in 2014, then stalled.
- Hedge funds typically charge "2 and 20" (2% management, 20% performance); liquid alt mutual funds typically can't charge performance fees and run 100 to 200 basis points all-in.
- 40 Act funds offer 1099 tax reporting; hedge fund LPs receive K-1s, which are more complex and arrive later.
- The illiquidity premium is, by structural definition, unavailable to a daily-liquid mutual fund.
- In the SEC staff's 2015 study of fund filings, about 91% of alternative mutual funds were authorized to use derivatives versus 73% of traditional mutual funds, though only about 14% reported actually doing so.

## Part 6: The bigger picture

The honest read on CAIA and mutual funds is this: the credential exists *because* mutual funds, ETFs, and the rest of the traditional public-markets toolkit aren't enough to explain how a modern institutional portfolio is built. The Yale Model, the rise of private equity as an asset class, the institutionalization of hedge funds, the explosion of private credit: none of these fit inside a 40 Act wrapper, and none of them are CFA core material.

But you can't analyze alternatives in a vacuum. Every allocation to a hedge fund is a decision *not* to put that capital into a long-only equity mutual fund. Every private credit allocation is a decision *not* to buy a high-yield bond ETF. Every real estate fund commitment is a decision *not* to own a publicly-traded REIT.

So mutual funds are everywhere in CAIA, not as the subject, but as the counterfactual. They're the answer to "what would you be doing instead if you weren't doing this?" And the more clearly you understand what mutual funds do well (cheap, liquid, transparent, simple) and where they fall short (no illiquidity premium, limited shorting, capped leverage, no access to private markets), the more clearly you can evaluate the alternatives that CAIA is actually about.

That's the lens. Hold it, and the curriculum makes a lot more sense.
